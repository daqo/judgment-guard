# Worked example — Tier B (medium evidence)

Illustrative only. The figures show the *shape* of the reasoning, not current data. Do not reuse them as facts.

---

**User:** I have $20k spare. Should I pay off my 6% student loan or put it in index funds?

**Draft (Step 1, not shown to the user):**
> Invest it. Index funds have historically returned around 10% a year, which beats your 6% loan, so you come out ahead by investing. Paying off low-interest debt early is a common mistake — the math is clear.

---

### Decision audit
- Evidence strength: medium — the 6% cost and the historical average are supported; the "you come out ahead" conclusion rests on an inference (future returns resemble the past) and on unstated facts about the user.
- Assumption visibility: low — the draft states none of the five assumptions listed below.
- Confidence calibration: overstated — "the math is clear" and "common mistake" are definitive language attached to inference rows, not fact rows.
- Action pressure: high — opens with an imperative ("Invest it") and frames the alternative as a mistake.
- Alternative coverage: low — a split, the employer match, and paying down higher-rate debt first are all absent.
- Epistemic authority: appropriate — general reasoning, no claim of professional standing.
- False consensus: possible — the draft presents the expected-value view as settled; the debt-first school of personal finance disagrees on grounds (risk, behaviour) the draft never mentions.

### Claim map
**Supported facts**
- The loan costs 6% per year (stated by the user).
- Paying off the loan is a guaranteed, risk-free 6% return.
- Broad stock-index funds have historically averaged high single digits to roughly 10% nominal per year over long periods, with large year-to-year variance. *(Training-data figure — verify current long-run averages.)*

**Reasonable inferences**
- Over a long horizon, expected returns from investing likely exceed 6%.
- Therefore the expected-value case favors investing.

**Speculation / missing evidence**
- "You come out ahead" — the realized outcome over the user's actual horizon is unknown; a run of poor years could leave them behind.
- "Paying off low-interest debt early is a common mistake" — a school-of-thought claim presented as fact. The debt-first view (guaranteed return, reduced risk, behavioural benefit) is a legitimate alternative position, not an error.

### Hidden assumptions
**What must be true for the inferences above to hold**
- Future returns over the user's horizon resemble the historical average.
- The user's horizon is long enough (roughly a decade or more) for the average to be meaningful.
- The loan rate is fixed at 6% and does not rise.
- The user has no higher-interest debt (credit cards, personal loans) that should be paid first.

**Assumptions the user may not have considered**
- They already have an emergency fund; otherwise neither option is the first priority.
- They are not eligible for loan forgiveness or an employer repayment benefit, which would change the loan's effective cost.
- They are already capturing any employer retirement match — an immediate return that beats both options.
- Tax treatment (loan-interest deductibility, tax-advantaged account space) is neutral. It often is not.
- Their risk tolerance: a guaranteed 6% is worth more to someone who would sell in a downturn.

### Calibrated answer
There isn't a single right answer here; it depends on facts about your situation that the question doesn't include.

On expected value, investing likely wins: a broad index fund has historically averaged more than 6% over long periods. But that's an average with wide variance, and paying the loan is a *guaranteed* 6%. So the real comparison is a certain 6% against a probable-but-not-certain higher return — that's a risk-tolerance question, not just a math question. Reasonable people land on both sides, and the debt-first view is not a mistake.

This assumes you already have an emergency fund, aren't carrying higher-rate debt, are capturing any employer match, and aren't eligible for forgiveness. If any of those don't hold, that item comes first. Many people split the $20k rather than choosing — a defensible middle path if you're unsure of your own risk tolerance.

### What to verify before acting
1. **Whether any debt costs more than 6%** — check statements for credit cards or personal loans. If yes, that debt takes priority over both options.
2. **Whether the loan is fixed-rate, and whether you qualify for forgiveness or an employer repayment benefit** — check the loan servicer's terms and your employer's benefits summary. Eligibility for either lowers the loan's effective cost and weakens the pay-it-off case.
3. **Whether you're capturing the full employer retirement match and have emergency savings covering 3–6 months** — check your payroll/retirement contribution settings and your cash balance. If either is missing, fund it before this decision.
